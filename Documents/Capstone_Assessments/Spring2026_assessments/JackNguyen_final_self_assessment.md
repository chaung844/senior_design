On my end, I was responsible for drafting UI elements, doing data-processing and coming up with deterministic ways to identify batch-matching as the model reconciles multiple charges. After making the project goals last Fall, I sensed that I work better as a supporting role for my teammates, doing smaller work that’s necessary for our project to take off and start. I drafted up the earlier front-end design using Figma, a software that I have close to zero experience in, but that process of figuring out how to use a new tool was very exciting at the time. Then I scrubbed through the data (receipts and bank statements) to write out the ground truth so that we would have  something to compare the agent’s performance against. And early on in the development process, we also ran into another problem of how to deal with receipts that are charged multiple times as multiple transactions on multiple dates, but they only show up in the bank statement as one singular line. I pitched and helped develop the idea of batch-matching the receipts if that was the case using its total price and comparing it against the amount that showed up in the statement and checking to see if the vendor and date matched. That felt especially satisfying because we managed to come up with a solution to a very complex problem that came up even before we started development.
I felt like I really expanded my technical skills, especially with bridging the gap between the UI and the more complex data models. I had some experience before, but I got much better at optimizing the data-processing workflows to make them more efficient. My main win was getting the deterministic batch-matching logic deployed, which ended up being the backbone for the model's accuracy. I did hit some roadblocks, though, especially with how messy and inconsistent the data from different receipts was, which meant we had to keep refining our scripts over and over. In the end though, I couldn’t be more happy with what we developed and I’m super excited to show it off at the expo.
